Review

Blood Meridian is considered McCarthy’s masterpiece. It bathes in violence. An occasional refrain on the Reading McCarthy podcast is, “no author has killed as many babies as McCarthy.” This is so. One coming to McCarthy with only this perception, and perhaps the images of a burntout country and ash from The Road, they probably think the books are reveling in that violence. That is not so.

The Kid comes out of Tennessee into the West and tumbles and starts problems until he falls in with Captain Glanton’s gang of scalphunters. Among that gang eventually are The Kid, Toadvine, Expriest Tobin, and several others. The Judge is the neonlit attraction. He arrives over and again from nowhere and leaves to nowhere and while he’s there he wanders around nude and glowing and pontificating. The Judge attracts a lot of attention from readers; maybe the expriest attracts just a little less. On this my second read, the expriest stood out the most and it is easy for the reader to imagine the Kid with the Judge on one shoulder and the Expriest on another whispering in his ears.

So much has been written about Blood Meridian. I don’t have anything of worth to add to that after only two reads. I was talking about the book with someone the other day, and they were surprised to hear it is not my favorite from McCarthy, and I said that it is not one I have great love for. It isn’t that I dislike the book. I think it is McCarthy’s second best following The Road, and it is undeniable. But I am not drawn to read it over and over again in the way that I am The Road, it does not speak to a part of me that is looking for something. Blood Meridian is about incarnations of evil and the capacity of humans for violence, and maybe a little about resisting or denying it.

In the morning they rode out to the south. Little was said, nor were they quarrelsome among themselves. In three days they would fall upon a band of peaceful Tiguas camped on the river and slaughter them every soul.

(Chapter XIII)

[A 10-year-old boy] was lying face down naked in one of the cubicles. Scattered about on the clay were great numbers of old bones. As if he like others before him had stumbled upon a place where something inimical lived. The squatters crowded in and stood about the corpse in silence. Soon they were conversing senselessly about the merits and virtues of the dead boy.

(Chapter IX)

Years after the main events in the book, The Kid (now called The Man) is wandering around the countryside and three youths join his fire. The youths are talking about cruelties and meanness and this pulls one of my favorite lines from The Kid/Man in the book:

He looked at them from one to the other. He reached and took up a stick and roused the fire with it and put the stick in the flames. You all like meanness? he said.

One of them doubts and contradicts everything The Man says, and eventually riles him up. The boy is taken away by the others at the warning of The Man. Later, the boy returns with a rifle. The Man shoots him down.

In this same section, another favorite:

This country was filled with violent children orphaned by war.

All of these are things that are interesting to me. And how can one argue with the scene of The Judge making gunpowder, taken straight from Paradise Lost?

There are rivetting passages and I will surely read it many more times, but I doubt it is ever a yearly thing for me. I think because where Blood Meridian is such, The Road is at its core a story about love.

There is a line that caught my attention this time that hasn’t in the past:

Whoever would seek out his history through what unraveling of loins and ledgerbooks must stand at last darkened and dumb at the shore of a void without terminus or origin and whatever science he might bring to bear upon the dusty primal matter blowing down out of the millennia will discover no trace of any ultimate atavistic egg by which to reckon his commencing.

(Chapter XXII, emphasis mine.)

Cormac loves the ledgerbooks. From The Road:

Do you think that your fathers are watching? That they weigh you in their ledgerbooks? Against what? There is no book and your fathers are dead in the ground.

(The Road, page 196)

So while this is not my favorite McCarthy or favorite book, it is clearly a great work and one I enjoyed reading. It’s a hard book to put down, even with the major violence.

An oft-asked question is, Who should direct an adaptation of Blood Meridian? Many would say that it should not be adapted, and I’m probably in that camp. My usual answer other than this, though, is my favorite director, David Lynch (I’m always thrilled when I see him mentioned in these conversations). Lynch was a director with a great understanding of and ability to utilize violence, rather than just making things look violent. He was able to make one of the most horrifying film scenes out of daylight, a dream, and a lady in makeup.

John Hillcoat is the director that adapted The Road, and he is usually mentioned. He has made allusions to such an adaptation over the past years. I hope this is not so. His adaptation of The Road fundamentally does not understand the text and is poorly acted and poorly made. It is probably the adaptive work I most revile. Occasionally one will see Ridley Scott, responsible for The Counselor (another quite poor film with a script from McCarthy that is unfortunately middling at best). Ridley’s hit rate is pretty low over the past ten years so let’s hope not. And also mentioned generally are the Coen Brothers, who, if they ever reunite, might do well. Blood Meridian has quite a few funny moments and that blend of cruelty and comedy might be well done by them. I recently saw Paul Thomas Anderson put forward, and I think he could do it well.

I like the book. It’s probably not one I’ll ever write a long essay about, but it is a gripping read.

Epilogue

  • In the down there is a man progressing over the plain by means of holes which he is making in the ground. He uses an implement with two handles and he chucks it into the hole and he enkindles the stone in the hole with his steel hole by hole striking the fire out of the rock which God has put there. On the plain behind him are the wanderers in search of bones and those who do not search and they move haltingly in the light like mechanisms whose movements are monitored with escapement and pallet so that they appear restrained by a prudence or reflectiveness which has no inner reality and they cross in their progress one by one that track of holes that runs to the rim of the visible ground and which seems less the pursuit of some continuance than the verification of a principle, a validation of sequence and causality as if each round and perfect hole owed its existence to the one before it there on that prairie upon which are the bones and the gatherers of bones and those who do not gather. He strikes fire in the hole and draws out his steel. Then they all move on again.
