Getting Lost

October 22, 2026 — Annie Ernaux

Table of Contents
  • Review
  • Notes

Review

Ernaux’s diaries from an affair with a married Russian diplomat, previously autofictionalized in Simple Passion. My book club had mixed thoughts. I am not to be trusted as Ernaux is my favorite living writer, but I loved the text. It is so familiar to me, the obsession and the nameless terror. Like SP, I see so much of myself in the worry, anxiety, and the self-decimating thoughts; the insane want to understand one’s meaning to another. As if understanding will salve your sores.

Ernaux describes waiting on phone calls from S. She descends into bleak darkness as time stretches further and further from last contact, only to be sundrenched at the first sign of attention. The phone call, the liaison. When he comes, she feels life is worth living for a while. And she knows that the glow is only temporary, and the darkness come back when he’s gone. She even discusses object permanence in children and the nameless terror when S disappears (so familiar, I’ve said, “it’s like I have no object permanence” to my therapist). And so this happens again and again with S. Their relationship can barely be called emotional, it is a tryst through and through. He shows up and they fuck, usually several times. Ernaux describes the positions, the feeling of his body, his sperm. Yet, she is infatuated with him, the world turns on his decision to stop by a phonebooth. She contemplates the unrequited nature of this relationship, the one-sidedness (in her mind), the paranoia she feels.

Folks in my book club began to tire of the dark-to-light-to-dark cycle that Ernaux describes over and over again. But this is a diary! It felt familiar to me, I think my journal would read similarly if I felt up to maintaining it. One person pointed out that she references the fall of the Berlin Wall and dedicates about three sentences to it amidst two-hundred pages of yearning.

Unfortunately, this was the introduction to Ernaux for some folks in the club, and this is not a good introductory text. We read The Years a little over a year ago, which was tremendously popular and much more accessible. Some wondered if this diary were contrived, to what extent Ernaux planned the publication not only of SP but of this text, and to what extent Ernaux subjected herself to misery and to this obsessive thinking for the purposes of art. It’s true that in her oeuvre the question of, “why do I write” comes up again and again. She asks it a few different ways here. In Simple Passion, she writes:

Sometimes I wonder if the purpose of my writing is to find out whether other people have done or felt the same things or, if not, for them to consider experiencing such things as normal. Maybe I would also like them to live out these very emotions in turn, forgetting that they had once read about them somewhere.
(*Simple Passion*, p50)

One person imagined Ernaux writing these diaries and having “a little voyeur” in the back of her mind, considering publication. I wrote this in the back of the book as, un petit voyeur, which I think would make for a not-so-bad Ernaux title. We are, after all, voyeurs, all of us. I hesitated to push against these questions because I felt myself taking the role of Ernaux Whisperer, repeatedly referencing her other works and events from her life, recurring themes. Yet I feel it unlikely that these are in any way contrived.

Some wondered about the ethics of publishing GL, suggesting that S is probably easy to identify. Probably so. GL released in 2001, several years after the fall of the Soviet Union (for which S worked as a diplomat). SL in 1991. In SL, S becomes A, and his figure is much more obscured. I find I am unbothered by potential identification, despite my self-interrogation. Do people have a right to their own lives, for the purposes of writing? Without a doubt. To what extent much the Others in our lives be obscured when we write? I think there are some basic good practices (changing names, perhaps changing locations). Yet, I think all is bound by time. Would I feel differently if I found this work, and its author, repulsive?

Anyway, as with all of Ernaux’s works, I feel there’s much to relate to, and it is full of empathy despite its clinical prose. One person in club said, “maybe this is for the completionists” (paraphrasing); maybe so. But I’m one of those, and so it worked well for me.
