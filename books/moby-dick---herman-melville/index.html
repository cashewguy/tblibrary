Sperm, sperm’s the play!

Moby-Dick

August 9, 2026 — Herman Melville

Table of Contents
  • Review
  • Notes

Review

Sometimes a classic is a classic for a reason. I remember picking up Moby-Dick sometime in elementary or maybe middle school and bouncing off of it instantly. Hardly surprising. I doubt I thought about the book at all between then and a few months ago, reading that Cormac McCarthy drew some inspiration for Blood Meridian from Melville’s work, and apparently this was McCarthy’s favorite novel. Not difficult to see why, or to see the parallels.

But perhaps the most surprising question I have after closing the book: Is Moby-Dick queer? I think there’s a pretty reasonable read that way. We start off with Ishmael sharing a bed with Queequeg, and Ishmael seems rather taken with his new friend, and quite happy to think himself almost as a wife to him (I believe Queequeg actually refers to him as such, though with some cultural meanings otherwise maybe). The sperm of it all certainly reads as homoerotic! I didn’t get particularly queer vibes from the book while reading, but I’ll admit that some has been a bit recontextualized as I read more about Melville and the man to whom this book is dedicated, Nathaniel Hawthorne. I’ve read a few articles now, including one I quite from Maria Popova, adapted from her book Figuring (which I purchased just after reading this article), which is pretty convincing to me (article: “Herman Melville’s Passionate, Beautiful, Heartbreaking Love Letters to Nathaniel Hawthorne”). Sent or not, I’ve written love letters, and spoken them into the loved’s ear, and I know them when I see them.

I did not expect to fall for the writing in the way that I did. So melodious is it at times that it’s hard to stay silent. I read much of the book aloud to my cat, the language being so pleasing to pronounce. The contents themselves remind me of Les Miserables, with the chapter-long digressions. Where Les Mis spends a hundred pages discussing Waterloo or argot, Melville discusses at exceptional length every minute detail of the whale’s anatomy, the kinds of whales, the churning of sperm, and more, and more, and more. I admit that some of Hugo’s digressions tuckered me out, but that did not happen here. Perhaps because Les Mis is some 1200 pages, almost double M-D.

Because I began the book with Blood Meridian in mind, it became difficult not to look for similarities and differences. Very often I thought of the Judge, and the environs while one being land and the other sea, are characterized similarly. There is a great deal of religious language, and both Ahab and the Judge clearly think of themselves as gods (perhaps Ahab moreso than the Judge, it’s been a few years since I read B-M). Several times, Ahab declares himself as having adopted the power of the gods, or having been a god, being ageless, etc. In one of the most gripping scenes, Ahab shouts out to a storm:

‘Oh! thou clear spirit of clear fire, whom on these seas I as Persian once did worship, till in the sacramental act so burned by thee, that to this hour I bear the scar; I know thee, thou clear spirit, and I now know that thy right worship is defiance. To neither love nor reverence wilt thou be kind; and e’en for hate thou canst but kill; and all are killed. No fearless fool now fronts thee. I own thy speechless, placeless power: but to the last gasp of my earthquake life will dispute its unconditional, unintegral mastery in me.’
(p. 676)

The next page:

‘All your oaths to hunt the white whale are as binding as mine . . . And that ye may know to what tune this heart beats: look ye here; thus I blow out the last fear!’ And with one blast of his breath he extinguished the flame.

It is difficult for me not to picture Ahab here as Poseidon with his trident. I had to remind myself over and over again that Ahab wielded a harpoon. There is so much to swim in and ponder.

But there is also a lot, a ton, to laugh at or along with. The opening chapters with Ishmael and Queequeg are a delight to read. It is hard, though, to beat the chapter that’s all about squeezing sperm. Beginning on page 562, chapter ‘A Squeeze of the Hand’ does nothing but make me cackle and gasp. I have not seen so much sperm written on the page since I was scribbling out erotic stories in high school. Let us enjoy them together:

It was our business to squeeze these lumps back into fluid. A sweet and unctuous duty! No wonder that in old times sperm was such a favourite cosmetic. Such a clearer! Such a sweetener! Such a softener! Such a delicious mollifier! After having my hands in it for only a few minutes, my fingers felt like eels, and began as it were to serpentine and spiralize. . . .
. . . I snuffed up that uncontaminated aroma, literally and truly, like the smell of spring violets; I declare to you, that for the time I lived as in a musky meadow; I forgot all about our horrible oath; in that inexpressible sperm, I washed my hands and my heart of it, I almost began to credit the old Paracelsian supersittion that sperm is of rare virtue in allaying the heat of anger; while bathing in that bath, I felt divinely free from all ill-will, or petulance, or malice, of any sort whatsoever.
Squeeze! squeeze! squeeze! all the morning long; I squeezed that sperm till I myself almost melted into it; I squeezed that sperm till a strange sort of insanity came over me, and I found myself unwittingly ssqueezing my co-labourers’ hands in it, mistaking their hands for the gentle globules. Such an abounding, affectionate, friendly, loving feeling did this avocation beget; that at last I was continually squeezing their hands, and looking up into their eyes sentimentally, as much as to say, Oh! my dear fellow beings, why should we longer cherish any social acerbities, or know the slightest ill-humour or envy! Come; let us squeeze hands all round; nay, let us all squeeze ourselves into each other; let us squeeze ourselves universally into the very milk and sperm of kindness.

How could one read this without bursting? It’s tremendous. I’m so pleased to have read this and really find it such a treat. For such a long book, it reads very fast and is “fun” by my definition the whole way through.
