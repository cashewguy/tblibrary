Au revoir, mon cher

Giovanni's Room

August 16, 2026 — James Baldwin

Table of Contents

Review

I first read Giovanni’s Room four years ago. I remember gripping it tightly the whole time, bent over and racing through it. Not 200 pages, Baldwin moves through the story so incisively that it really does feel like the drop of a guillotine.

I do not remember detesting David, our narrator, so much on my first read. Depressed moody people may be able to relate to either being or wanting to be in an empty home with a big bottle of wine and looking moodily out of windows (certain I can), but David does begin to feel like an alien. In his early experience with Joey, he vanishes out of insecurity and fear. That’s understandable—coming of age, a much different era than we’re in now. Even in our current era, I can remember first realizing I’d fallen for a boy in one of my high school classes, and that was terrifying.

With Giovanni, something much colder takes place. David finds him beautiful, there is clearly lust (which, unfortunately, we don’t get quite enough of, but let’s attribute that to time—there is more straight sex than queer here), and David enjoys being with him. But I think there is something a little worrying:

And I realized that such childishness was fantastic at my age and the happiness out of which it sprang yet more so; for that moment I really loved Giovanni, who had never seemed more beautiful than he was that afternoon. And, watching his face, I realized that it meant much to me that I could make his face so bright. I saw that I might be willing to give a great deal not to lose that power. And I felt myself flow towards him, as a river rushes when ice breaks up.
(page 83, emphasis mine.)

When it is so crystalized, the realizing moment of love is an incredible clarity. I am not sure there is any better feeling than recognizing that you can make someone you love laugh, or make them recognize that they feel good when they’re with you. Maybe if they recognize it themselves and point it out; that is pretty good. But when David contemplates this, what he feels attachment to is not necessarily Giovanni’s beautiful face or the sound of his voice, but the power that he has over Giovanni, the power to elicit those things. Possibly I am too focused on this one word, power. In another book, with a character that behaved differently, I would be inclined to ignore it as just a way of saying, “I loved how I could make him feel and laugh,” which is perfectly pleasant. But David does not behave warmly towards Giovanni, not really.

In his thought, he expresses conflict, confusion, maybe regret. But at no point does he ever treat Giovanni as a person, and none of the conflict or anxiety in any way materializes in behavior. “Now she would be coming back and my life with Giovanni would be finished. It would be something that had happened to me once” (page 94). Ten pages after realizing his great love for Giovanni, he is dispassionate in the love’s approaching end. On finding Giovanni in a state of crisis, David does nothing but think of how messy the room is, how annoyed he is to be dealing with everything.

He doesn’t say anything to Giovanni, he disappears without a word. David behaves hatefully towards him.

Later, when he does finally appear in Giovanni’s room to draw things to some formal close—as if they were emotionless fuckbuddies—all he has for Giovanni is his own insecurity and self-hatred:

“Yes” I said, wearily, “I can have a life with her.” I stood up. I was shaking. “What kind of life can we have in this room?—this filthy little room. What kind of life can two men have together, anyway? All this love you talk about—isn’t it just that you want to be made to feel strong? You want to go out and be the big laborer and bring home the money, and you want me to stay here and wash the dishes and cook the food and clean this miserable closet of a room and kiss you when you come in through that door and lie with you at night and be your little girl. That’s what you want. That’s what you mean and that’s all you mean when you say you love me. You say I want to kill you. What do you think you’ve been doing to me?”
(page 142.)

Each and every thing David lays at Giovanni’s feet is his own insecurity and self-contempt. Giovanni never talks about feeling strong, never talks about wanting to be some big laborer, has no sense at all of wanting to coop David up like a canary. Giovanni’s retort is what it should be, if he wanted a girl, he’d have one.

In the end, all he has for Giovanni is dread at seeing him around the quarter (page 147, “And from time to time, around the quarter, I ran into Giovanni. I dreaded seeing him. . .”). In the weeks that Giovanni is imprisoned, we see David abscond from his fiancé to bounce around on a sailor. The lives of each person that feel love towards him, he ruins. Does he feel anything?

Much has been written of love turning to hatred, of the heart growing cold with the death of love. It is a remarkable process. It is far more terrible than anything I have ever read about it, more terrible than anything I will ever be able to say.
(page 158)

It is difficult for me to accept that David loved Giovanni. I cannot imagine loving someone that is being executed and having such a dissociated reaction. I am actually quite sure that if I could do nothing about it, I wouldn’t be able to allow myself to outlive him. I don’t hate myself any more than David does himself. At the start of the novel, Baldwin writes that people believing themselves to be strong-willed are experts at self-deception. David has caged himself with masculinity and perception and fear, and this cage is a dissociative shield. He says of Joey, all he had to do would be to protest, and he’d have stayed. Decades later he thinks of Giovanni as he walks away that he wants to turn around and go back, but that with each step it becomes more and more impossible. He calls his mind an anaesthetized wound. And so, in some ways, perhaps David’s death pre-dates Giovanni, maybe David never lived at all, outside of that moment of Giovanni’s laughter.

No matter how it seems now, I must confess: I loved him. I do not think that I will ever love anyone like that again. And this might be a great relief if I did not also know that, when the knife has fallen, Giovanni, if he feels anything will feel relief.

(page 112.)
